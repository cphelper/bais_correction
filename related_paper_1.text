1. Introduction
Sea surface waves alter sea surface roughness and surface winds and influence the exchange of mass, momentum, and heat at the air-sea interface (Hwang, 2011). Huge waves under extreme wind conditions usually cause
severe coastal and ship disasters, which have led to thousands of deaths and extensive property losses in the past
two decades (Chang & Mori, 2021; Möller et al., 2008). Therefore, providing accurate wave height forecasts is
necessary and urgent (Alves et al., 2013). However, significant bias still exist in numerical wave models (Campos
et al., 2018, 2019) due mainly to simplified physical processes, inaccurate initial conditions, and limited grid
resolution (Cannon, 2018; Giorgi & Bi, 2000; Hack et al., 2006). Bias correction was proposed to mitigate the
bias in numerical models to fit the observations better (Soriano et al., 2019). Bias correction techniques for results
from numerical models include linear regression, quantile mapping, cumulative distribution functions, Kalman
filter, and so on (Erickson et al., 2012; Kalman, 1960; Li, Feng, et al., 2019; Thrasher et al., 2012).
Driven by big data and the improvement of computing power, machine learning, especially deep learning, has
developed rapidly in recent years and has been applied to many different fields. Recently, machine learning has
been applied to model output statistics, bias correction, and statistical downscaling, providing new ideas and
methods for weather forecasts (Guan, 2020; Li, Yu et al., 2019; O’Donncha et al., 2018; Rasp & Lerch, 2018;
Reichstein et  al., 2019; Vandal et  al., 2018). Significant wave height (SWH), the average wave height (from
trough to crest) of the highest one-third of the waves, is often used to represent the size of swell and wind
waves in wave models. Machine learning can effectively identify and reduce the bias in SWH nowcast and
reconstruction (Alexandre et  al.,  2015; Guan,  2020; O’Donncha et  al.,  2018). Ellenson et  al.  (2020) used a
bagged regression tree to correct the SWH bias forecasted by WAVEWATCH3 (WW3) for five stations along the
California-Oregon border in the United States. They reduced the SWH Root Mean Squared Errors (RMSEs) from
0.33 to 0.29 m in summer and from 0.58 to 0.48 m in winter. Note the performance improvement in predicting
Abstract Ocean waves, especially extreme waves, are vital for air-sea interaction and shipping. However,
current wave models still have significant biases. Based on a numerical wave model and a deep learning model,
a BU-Net by adding batch normalization layers to a U-Net, we accurately predict the significant wave height
(SWH) of the Northwest Pacific Ocean. For each day in 2017–2021, we conducted a 3-day hindcast experiment
using WAVEWATCH3 (WW3) to obtain the SWH forecasts at lead times of 24, 48, and 72 hr, forced by GFS
real-time forecast surface winds. After using BU-Net, the mean Root Mean Squared Errors (RMSEs) of the
SWH forecast from WW3 at lead times of 24, 48, and 72 hr are reduced by 40%, 38%, and 30%, respectively.
During typhoon passages, the drop percentages of RMSEs all exceed 20% for three lead times. Therefore,
combining numerical models and deep learning is very promising in wave forecasting.
Plain Language Summary Data-driven deep learning is widely used in geoscience. A new deep
learning bias correction method, BU-Net, is proposed to correct the significant wave height forecast over the
Northwest Pacific Ocean. It is demonstrated that BU-Net improves the forecast performance over four seasons
and performs well under extreme weather conditions such as typhoons. This new deep learning bias correction
method provides a successful approach that can also be applied to the routine forecast of other atmospheric and
ocean phenomena in future.

In addition to forecasts at buoy stations, the bias correction based on convolutional neural networks (CNN) facilitates the spatial correction and grid predictions by extracting the spatial connections and dependences (Deshmukh
et al., 2016; Han et al., 2021; Lecun et al., 1998; F. Wang & Tian, 2022). CNN is one of the most extensively
implemented deep learning algorithms and is often used in image segmentation, target detection, image denoising, and image super-resolution (Dong et al., 2014; He et al., 2016; Long et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2017). CNN
has powerful image processing capabilities that can obtain abstract features from massive high-dimensional
geospatial data. Bias correction can be viewed as image segmentation, typically transforming an input image into
another. Therefore, CNN has become a valuable candidate for bias correction and forecasts. Han et al. (2021)
corrected the 2-m temperature, 2-m relative humidity, 10-m wind speed, and 10-m wind direction in northeastern
China from ECMWF-IFS data sets using a deep learning method called correction U-Net (CU-Net). U-Net, one
of the fully convolutional networks, is the simplest but most successful method in image segmentation (Siddique
et al., 2021). It is an encoder-decoder model with a skip connection consisting of downsampling and upsampling
processes, resulting in a U-shaped architecture. CU-Net outperforms the anomaly numerical correction method,
which demonstrates the advantages of U-Net (Ronneberger et al., 2015). F. Wang and Tian (2022) used the Super
Resolution Deep Residual Network (SRDRN), which is mainly composed of residual blocks and upsampling
blocks with CNN and batch normalization (BN) layers (Ioffe & Szegedy, 2015), to correct the temperature of the
southern parts of the United States and the Gulf of Mexico from 20 state-of-the-art coupled general circulation
models. The SRDRN could reduce the RMSE by about 8%–20%, while the standard quantile delta mapping
(QDM) approach only reduced the RMSE by 0.6%–11%.
The Northwest Pacific Ocean (NWPO) is one region with frequent human activities. The NWPO region has a
typical monsoon climate. The average wind speed at the sea surface in the region has an apparent seasonal cycle.
Within the NWPO region, extreme winds frequently occur in the South China Sea, the Taiwan Strait, and the
seas east of Japan (Ding & Liu, 2008). The upper ocean circulation in NWPO is dominated by the Kuroshio
current, which is a powerful western boundary current that transports warm equatorial water poleward (Mizuno
& White, 1983). In particular, this region suffers from intense typhoons and huge sea surface waves (Chu, 2004;
B. Wang et al., 2010, 2013). A few studies were devoted to correcting the SWH products from numerical wave
models (Ellenson et al., 2020; Lemos et al., 2020; O’Donncha et al., 2018). However, there is still a lack of using
the spatial bias correction method to correct bias in the SWH forecast of NWPO from numerical wave models.
In this study, we propose a deep learning-based bias correction method, that is, BU-Net, by inserting BN layers
into the U-Net, for correcting SWH in NWPO from WW3. BN improves the correction performance of U-Net. In
particular, we have used wind forcing, wave direction, and wave period to train BU-Net. The BU-Net successfully
considers spatial connections and dependencies among meteorological variables and wave parameters. For lead
times of 24, 48, and 72 hr, we construct three independent BU-Nets for them.
The rest of this paper is organized as follows. Data and methods are described in Section 2. Section 3 presents
the results of the new bias correction method. Finally, Section 4 provides the summary and concluding remarks.

2.2. BU-Net Architecture
The bias correction model is the BU-Net, by adding BN layers to the U-Net proposed by Ronneberger et al. (2015).
The BU-Net differs from U-Net in two aspects: (a) A BN layer is included between each convolutional layer and
the ReLU activation function in the Convolutional Block (Conv-Block). BN confines the variance of input data to
a specific range so that the latter layer does not need to constantly adapt to the input changes, allowing each layer
to learn independently, helping the neutral network become faster and more stable. (b) Using the same padding
method in the convolutional layer ensures the same input and output sizes, which is necessary for bias correction.
Figure 1 shows the structure and the data flow of BU-Net. The size of an input image is (80, 80, 6), representing
the width of 80, the height of 80, and 6 channels. Six channels represent the input features, that is, the six output
variables from WW3 (Table S1 in Supporting Information S1). The basic module of BU-Net is the Conv-Block,
which consists of a 3 × 3 two-dimensional convolutional layer, a BN layer, and a ReLU activate function. The
length and width of the input and output of Conv-Block remain unchanged, and the number of convolution
kernels determines the number of output channels. The five encoding processes on the left continuously compress
the length and width of the feature map, enhancing higher-order features. After going through the decoding
processes on the right, including upsampling and skipping connection, we can obtain a feature map of (80, 80,
64) and then reduce the channel to 1 through a 1 × 1 convolutional layer as the output of the model. We use the
mean squared error (Equation 1) as the loss function of the BU-Net. To prevent the results from being influenced
by the land points with no waves, we assign a weight of 0 to each land point when calculating the loss function.

maximal time step of WW3 experiments is 3,600 s, while the six output variables at 00:00 UTC of each day are
used for bias correction. Meanwhile, the significant height of combined wind waves and swell data at 00:00 UTC
of each day from ERA5 (Hersbach et al., 2018) is viewed as the ground truth of the bias correction module. We

32, respectively.

3.2. Forecast Performance of BU-Net During Typhoon Passages
The forecast performance of WW3 over NWPO has the most significant variability in summer, mainly related
to the frequent typhoon passages (Figure S1 in Supporting Information S1). According to the National Meteorological Centre (NMC), CMA (http://typhoon.nmc.cn/web.html), eight typhoons (Table S4 in Supporting Information S1) passed through the NWPO region in 2021. Here we define the typhoon passage time as the dates
when a typhoon center is located in the study area. The raw SWH RMSEs against ERA5 over the NWPO region
during typhoon passages are 0.55, 0.67, and 0.81 m at forecast lead times of 24, 48, and 72 hr (Table S5 in
Supporting Information S1). After using the BU-Net-based bias correction, the SWH RMSEs against ERA5 are
reduced to 0.30, 0.39, and 0.53 m. Correspondingly, the drop percentages are 45%, 42%, and 35%, indicating that
BU-Nets still work under extreme weather conditions. However, ERA5 shows systematic bias compared with
buoy observations or altimeter measurements (Bidlot et al., 2019), especially under extreme wind conditions.
We use a gridded multi-mission merged satellite SWH product (WAVE_GLO_WAV_L4_SWH_NRT_OBSERVATIONS_014_003, Mertz et al., 2019) to verify our model's performance under extreme wind conditions. The
RMSEs of WW3 SWH against the satellite measurements are 0.89, 0.79, and 1.26 m at three different lead times,
respectively (Table S5 in Supporting Information S1). After bias correction, the RMSEs against the satellite
measurements are reduced to 0.70, 0.61, and 0.93 m, corresponding to drop percentages of 21%, 23%, and 26%,
respectively. It demonstrates that the deep-learning bias correction model trained by ERA5 still works when evaluating against the satellite measurements. However, the improvement ratios are smaller than that of the RMSEs
estimated with respect to ERA5.
Due to the use of BU-Net-based bias correction, the SWH RMSEs over the NWPO region at all three forecast
lead times are reduced during the passage times of six out of eight typhoons (Figures 4a–4c). The exceptional
cases are typhoon Surigae and Choi-wan. After using bias correction, the forecast SWH RMSE against ERA5
during the passage of typhoon Surigae is reduced by 24% at a lead time of 24 hr (Figure 4a) but increased by
32% and 27% at lead times of 48 and 72 hr (Figures 4b and 4c). When comparing to the satellite measurements,
the forecast SWH RMSEs are reduced by 6.8% and 33% at lead times of 24 and 72 hr (Figures 4a and 4c) but
increased by 6.3% at a lead time of 48 hr (Figure 4b). During the passage of typhoon Choi-wan, the forecast
SWH RMSEs against ERA5 are reduced by 32%, 46%, and 27% at the three lead times (Table S4 in Supporting
Information S1). When comparing to the satellite measurements, the forecast SWH RMSEs during the passage
of typhoon Choi-wan are reduced by 17% and 28% at the lead times of 48 and 72 hr (Figures 4b and 4c), but the
forecast SWH RMSE at the lead time of 24 hr is the same as the RMSE without bias correction (Figure 4a). The
possible causes for the bad performance of BU-Net in correcting SWH during typhoon Surigae are the short duration of this typhoon (only 2 days) and the center of this typhoon being mainly located in the southernmost parts
of the study area, which means that the BU-net may perform worse in the boundary areas than the inner areas.
Note that the relatively small number of typhoon samples in the training data set is also a possible cause for the
poor performance of the BU-Net during the passage of typhoon Surigae.
In the other six cases, the SWH RMSEs against both ERA5 and the satellite measurements show comparatively

4. Summary and Concluding Remarks
This paper proposed a deep learning-based bias correction method for correcting SWH forecast over the NWPO
region from numerical wave model WW3. The new deep learning-based bias correction method, that is, BU-Net,
is constructed by adding batch normalization layers to the U-Net. Results show that the BU-Net converges quickly
and generalizes well on the testing data set. The BU-Net essentially improves the forecast performance of SWH
over the NWPO region at lead times of 24, 48, and 72 hr in terms of reducing the SWH RMSE and increasing the correlations with observations. Further, the BU-Net improves the forecast performance of SWH in all
four seasons and is still valid during typhoon passages. Under extreme wind conditions related to typhoons, the
BU-Net corrects the overestimated huge waves predicted by WW3, significantly improving the forecast performance. However, the BU-Net performs poorly when the typhoon is in the boundary areas of the NWPO region
and has a short lifetime.
We have conducted a series of sensitivity experiments regarding the performance of the BU-Net method as a
function of the length of the training period. It is revealed that a longer training period does lead to better forecast
performance. However, a training period of 4 years is long enough to obtain relatively good performance (Table
S6 in Supporting Information S1). We have also conducted a series of sensitivity experiments regarding the
performance of the BU-Net method as a function of the number of input variables for the BU-Net. After removing any variables from the six input variables used for training the model, the BU-Net model performs worse in
predicting the SWH over NWPO (Table S7 in Supporting Information S1).
Compared to U-Net, BU-Net improves the forecast performance of WW3 at lead times of 24, 48, and 72 hr over
the NWPO region by about 8%. In this study, the BU-Net has been proved to be a suitable bias correction method
for correcting the bias of SWH forecast over the NWPO region. The time for a 3-day forecast using WW3 is about
9 min at the cost of 92 CPU cores, and that for preprocessing and BU-Net-based correction is less than 1 min once
the BU-Net model is built. Therefore, using the new approach proposed in this study to predict the ocean waves
in the NWPO region is very efficient. In the future, the BU-Net-based wave forecast method will be tested and
used operationally in Marine Forecasting Administration.
Changing Conv-2d in BU-Net into Conv-3d, taking the wave autocorrelation in the time direction into consideration, may further improve the forecast performance. In the future, BU-Net can be applied in the bias correction
of other important wave parameters like wave period, and discontinuous variables such as precipitation and the
joint bias correction of winds and waves.
