\begin{abstract}
Accurate projections of sea level anomaly (SLA) and significant wave height (SWH) are critical for coastal risk assessment under climate change. CMIP6 climate model projections exhibit systematic biases relative to satellite altimetry observations for both variables. This paper presents a comprehensive machine learning framework for (i) deterministic bias correction using Random Forest regression, and (ii) probabilistic prediction interval estimation using Tube Loss based neural networks, validated on two distinct ocean variables. For SLA over the high-latitude region (60°--80°N, 50°--110°E), the Random Forest correction reduces RMSE by 44.2\% (from 0.1037m to 0.0578m) with 94.7\% bias elimination. For SWH over the Indian Ocean (50°--110°E, 70°S--70°N) using COWCLIP-derived CMIP6 projections, the correction reduces RMSE by 33.2\% (from 0.6103m to 0.4080m) with $R^2=0.88$. For probabilistic forecasting, the Tube Loss neural network with conformal calibration achieves 92--93\% coverage for 95\% prediction intervals on both datasets. Cross-variable comparison demonstrates that the framework generalizes across ocean variables, with Tube Loss consistently producing narrower raw intervals than standard quantile decomposition (QD) loss while achieving comparable calibrated coverage.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%=====================================================================

Sea level rise and changes in ocean wave climate are among the most consequential impacts of anthropogenic climate change, with direct implications for coastal flooding, erosion, and maritime infrastructure planning~\cite{church2013}. Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6) Earth System Models provide projections of sea surface height (SSH) and significant wave height (SWH) under various Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs). However, both variables exhibit systematic biases relative to satellite observations~\cite{lemos2020}. For SWH, a recent 8-model CMIP6-derived wave climate ensemble~\cite{morim2024} validated against satellite altimeters showed improved but still imperfect agreement, while COWCLIP studies~\cite{cowclip2023} revealed substantial regional biases in projected extreme wave indices over the Indian Ocean.

Classical bias correction methods (delta method, empirical quantile mapping) are univariate and assume stationary bias structures~\cite{cannon2018}. Recent machine learning approaches have demonstrated superior performance for both SLA and SWH by exploiting spatial, temporal, and state-dependent bias patterns~\cite{reichstein2019}. For SWH specifically, deep learning methods have achieved 13--46\% MAE reductions using trajectory GRU networks~\cite{wang2024swh}, while hybrid XGBoost models reduced spectral wave height errors by 33.3\% in operational forecasting~\cite{vanvledder2023}. However, most existing work focuses on deterministic point predictions, leaving uncertainty quantification for climate projection correction underexplored.

This paper makes three contributions: (1)~a Random Forest deterministic bias correction framework validated on both CMIP6 SLA and SWH; (2)~a Tube Loss~\cite{anand2024} based neural network for probabilistic prediction interval estimation, benchmarked against standard quantile decomposition (QD) loss; and (3)~a cross-variable comparison demonstrating that the framework generalizes across ocean variables with consistent performance. The Tube Loss function, recently proposed for prediction interval estimation and applied to wind speed forecasting~\cite{anand2024tube_wind}, offers theoretical coverage guarantees and allows trading off interval width versus calibration through a single optimization~\cite{anand2024}.

\subsection{Proposed Workflow Overview}

Fig.~\ref{fig:workflow} illustrates the complete end-to-end workflow of our proposed framework, which proceeds through six stages:

\textbf{Stage~1 --- Data Acquisition.} We ingest two complementary datasets: (a)~Level-4 gridded Sea Level Anomaly from multi-mission satellite altimetry (CMEMS \texttt{dt\_global\_twosat\_phy\_l4}), integrating measurements from SARAL/AltiKa, Sentinel-3A/B, Jason-3, and CryoSat-2 on a 0.25° rectilinear grid at monthly resolution (72~files spanning 2015--2020); and (b)~CMIP6 sea surface height above geoid (\texttt{zos}) from MPI-ESM1-2-LR under SSP2-4.5 on a curvilinear ocean grid ($13 \times 23$ points). Both are constrained to the study domain of 60°--80°N, 50°--110°E.

\textbf{Stage~2 --- Preprocessing \& Feature Engineering.} The satellite data is spatially subsetted to the Region of Interest and resampled to monthly means. The CMIP6 \texttt{zos} field is converted to a sea level anomaly by subtracting the 2015--2018 temporal mean ($\text{SLA}(t) = \text{ZOS}(t) - \overline{\text{ZOS}}_{2015\text{--}2018}$). The curvilinear model grid is then regridded to the observation grid using a \texttt{cKDTree} nearest-neighbour interpolation, expanding from $13 \times 23$ to $80 \times 240$ grid points. A tabular feature matrix is constructed by flattening across space and time, yielding 23,524 valid samples with four features per sample: \texttt{model\_sla} (the raw CMIP6 prediction), \texttt{lat} (latitude for north-south bias patterns), \texttt{lon} (longitude for east-west and coastal effects), and \texttt{month} (1--12 for seasonal bias cycles). Samples with missing values are removed.

\textbf{Stage~3 --- Temporal Data Splitting.} The data is split chronologically to simulate application to future unseen periods: training on 2015--2017 (11,808 samples, 50.2\%), calibration on 2018 (3,734 samples, 15.9\%), and testing on 2019--2020 (7,982 samples, 33.9\%). The calibration set is held out exclusively for conformal calibration of prediction intervals and is never used during model training.

\textbf{Stage~4A --- Deterministic Correction.} A Random Forest Regressor (50 trees, max depth 10, scikit-learn) is trained on the 2015--2017 training set to learn the mapping $(\text{model\_sla}, \text{lat}, \text{lon}, \text{month}) \rightarrow \text{obs\_sla}$, where \texttt{obs\_sla} is the satellite altimeter observation. The trained model is serialized and applied to the test set to produce corrected SLA predictions.

\textbf{Stage~4B --- Probabilistic Prediction Intervals.} A feed-forward neural network (PyTorch) with a shared trunk (128$\to$64$\to$32 units, ReLU, 10\% dropout) and three output heads (\emph{base}, \emph{half-width} via softplus, \emph{asymmetry} via sigmoid) is trained using the Tube Loss function~\cite{anand2024} to produce upper and lower prediction interval bounds. The architecture guarantees $\hat{f}_1 \geq \hat{f}_2$ through the parameterization $\hat{f}_1 = \text{base} + \text{softplus}(h_w)(1+\sigma(a))$ and $\hat{f}_2 = \text{base} - \text{softplus}(h_w)(2-\sigma(a))$. Training uses Adam optimizer (lr=$10^{-3}$) with ReduceLROnPlateau scheduling and early stopping. An identical architecture trained with QD (dual pinball) loss~\cite{pearce2018} serves as the baseline.

\textbf{Stage~5 --- Conformal Calibration \& Evaluation.} The raw Tube Loss intervals are calibrated using split conformal prediction~\cite{vovk2005} on the held-out 2018 calibration set. Nonconformity scores $s_i = \max(\hat{f}_2(\mathbf{x}_i) - y_i, \; y_i - \hat{f}_1(\mathbf{x}_i))$ are computed for each calibration sample; the $q$-th quantile of sorted scores yields an additive margin $\hat{q}$ that expands the intervals to $[\hat{f}_2(\mathbf{x}) - \hat{q}, \; \hat{f}_1(\mathbf{x}) + \hat{q}]$, providing distribution-free coverage guarantees. Both deterministic and probabilistic outputs are evaluated on the independent 2019--2020 test set.

\textbf{Stage~6 --- Results \& Outputs.} The framework produces: (i)~bias-corrected SLA fields with 44.2\% RMSE reduction and 94.7\% bias elimination; (ii)~calibrated 90\% and 95\% prediction intervals with PICP of 89.3\% and 92.3\% respectively; (iii)~diagnostic visualizations including scatter plots, time series with PI bands, spatial bias maps, monthly coverage analysis, and QD vs.\ Tube Loss comparisons.

\section{Related Work}
%=====================================================================

Bias correction of climate model projections and uncertainty quantification for sea level have been active research areas, spanning classical statistical methods, modern machine learning approaches, and probabilistic forecasting frameworks. We organize the literature into five categories and identify the specific gaps that our work addresses.

\subsection{Classical Statistical Bias Correction}

The simplest bias correction method is the \textbf{delta method}, which applies a constant additive or multiplicative shift derived from the historical period. While widely used for temperature and precipitation~\cite{lemos2020}, this method assumes stationary bias and cannot capture non-linear, state-dependent, or spatially varying errors~\cite{cannon2018}. Lemos et~al.~\cite{lemos2020} applied a modified quantile mapping to CMIP5 projected wave climate, achieving 10--20\% RMSE reductions for significant wave height, but noted that the delta method's stationarity assumption degrades under climate change.

\textbf{Empirical Quantile Mapping (EQM)} corrects the full distribution by mapping model quantiles to observed quantiles. A comprehensive intercomparison of EQM, Detrended Quantile Mapping (DQM), and Quantile Delta Mapping (QDM) across 11 CMIP6 GCMs on six continents found that EQM achieved the best overall RMSE of 0.30 for precipitation with the lowest uncertainty (variance = 0.0027)~\cite{intercomp2025}. \textbf{QDM}~\cite{cannon2018} preserves relative trends better in projection periods, but a recent ``perfect model'' experiment~\cite{qm_future2025} demonstrated that standard quantile mapping introduces biases in means, standard deviations, and extremes when applied to future climate scenarios, failing to capture the climate change signal. All these methods are univariate, treat each grid point independently, and cannot exploit spatial or temporal covariates.

For sea level specifically, bias correction has received less attention than for temperature and precipitation. The Ensemble Time-series Quantile Mapping (ETQM) approach~\cite{gupta2025} combines multiple methods to correct CMIP6 outputs over the Indian Ocean, reducing extremes biases by 1.5--4.5\% for temperature. However, no comparable quantile mapping study has targeted sea level anomaly with the RMSE reductions we achieve.

\subsection{Machine Learning for Ocean and Sea Level Bias Correction}

Machine learning methods have demonstrated clear advantages over classical approaches for ocean variable correction. We review the key contributions organized by target variable and methodology.

\subsubsection{Sea Level Applications}

Doganay et~al.~\cite{doganay2023} developed a state-dependent ML bias correction for sea level applied to an ensemble of downscaled regional climate projections over the Swedish Baltic coast, producing 2,600+ model-years of corrected hourly sea levels. Their method conditions corrections on the model's internal state rather than output distributions, improving upon classical approaches on standard skill scores. However, this method provides only deterministic corrections without uncertainty quantification.

Pasula and Subramani~\cite{pasula2025} applied deep learning (UNet, BiLSTM, ConvLSTM) to correct CMIP6 projections of sea surface temperature (SST) and dynamic sea level (DSL) in the Bay of Bengal. Starting from raw CMIP6 errors of 1.2°C (SST) and 1.1m (DSL) against ORAS5 reanalysis, their deep learning approach reduced RMSE by 0.15°C for SST and 0.3m for DSL compared to the Equi-Distant CDF (EDCDF) statistical baseline, representing approximately 15\% improvement. Our method achieves a substantially higher 44.2\% RMSE reduction, though we note the domains and variables differ.

\subsubsection{Sea Surface Height Reconstruction}

Martin et~al.~\cite{martin2023} used deep learning to synthesize satellite altimetry and SST observations for SSH anomaly mapping. Their approach achieved 17\% lower RMSE than optimal interpolation in the Gulf Stream Extension region and resolved spatial scales 30\% smaller than conventional methods. This work targets SSH reconstruction from observations rather than climate model bias correction, but demonstrates the potential of neural networks for improving SSH products.

\subsubsection{Ocean General Circulation Model Correction}

Guillou et~al.~\cite{guillou2025} embedded neural networks directly into the NEMO v4 ocean model to correct sea surface biases by learning state-dependent errors in air-sea heat fluxes. The pre-trained network uses atmospheric forcing, ocean state, and stratification indices as predictors, with wind forcing emerging as the most important variable. The corrections showed positive impacts beyond the training period for both hindcasts and predictions, demonstrating transferability across time periods.

\subsubsection{SWH Bias Correction and Forecasting}

Significant wave height correction has received substantial attention due to its importance for maritime safety and coastal engineering. Wang et~al.~\cite{wang2024swh} developed a trajectory GRU deep learning method for real-time SWH bias correction of ECMWF-IFS forecasts, achieving MAE reductions of 13--46\% across seasons using a novel pixel-switch loss function that focuses on pixels with large biases. A Transformer-enhanced UNet (TransUNet)~\cite{transunet2024} achieved 21.55\% RMSE reduction for 24-hour SWH forecasts, with 87.81\% of corrected errors within $\pm$0.2m. Chapman et~al.~\cite{chapman2025} used CNN-based state-dependent corrections achieving 25--35\% improvement for atmospheric variables.

For wave climate projections specifically, the COWCLIP project~\cite{cowclip2023} examined extreme wave height indices over the Indian Ocean using multi-model ensembles under RCP4.5/8.5, finding substantial projected changes in rough wave days over the Arabian Sea and eastern tropical Indian Ocean. Morim et~al.~\cite{morim2024} published an 8-model CMIP6-derived wave climate ensemble validated against satellite altimeters and ERA5, showing improved but still biased performance compared to CMIP5-forced ensembles.

\subsubsection{Tree-based Methods for Ocean Variables}

Ellenson et~al.~\cite{ellenson2020} used bagged regression trees to correct WaveWatch III SWH forecasts, reducing RMSE from 0.58m to 0.48m (17\% improvement) along the US West Coast, with corrections transferable across buoy locations. Van~Vledder et~al.~\cite{vanvledder2023} applied hybrid XGBoost models for operational wave forecasting in the Dutch North Sea, achieving 33.3\% reduction in spectral wave height errors. Random forest and gradient boosting trees have been shown to outperform neural networks for SWH correction, with gradient boosting achieving $\sim$20\% lower RMSE~\cite{james2020}. Alfaris et~al.~\cite{alfaris2024} compared XGBoost and Random Forest for ocean current temperature prediction, while XGBoost corrected Indian Ocean pCO$_2$ with $\sim$40\% RMSE improvement~\cite{pco2_2025}. Our Random Forest achieves 33.2\% RMSE reduction for SWH, consistent with these findings.

\subsubsection{Multi-decadal Sea Level Prediction}

Hammoud et~al.~\cite{hammoud2024} applied neural networks to multi-decadal sea level prediction using CESM2 large ensembles and satellite altimeter data, achieving $R^2 > 0.9$ at 2° resolution with 30-year lead times. Their approach provides uncertainty estimates via spectral clustering but does not produce calibrated prediction intervals.

\subsection{Uncertainty Quantification for Sea Level and Ocean Forecasting}

The need for probabilistic predictions with calibrated uncertainty has been increasingly recognized in the ocean and climate communities.

\subsubsection{Deep Ensemble Methods}

Chen et~al.~\cite{patel2024} developed an LSTM Deep Ensemble (LSTM-DE) for real-time significant wave height prediction, achieving $R^2 > 0.9$ and demonstrating that uncertainty calibration improved prediction interval quality by over 50\%. Li et~al.~\cite{li2025} applied CNN-BiLSTM hybrid models with modal decomposition for forecasting SWH intervals along China's coast. These approaches use computationally expensive ensemble training, whereas our method produces prediction intervals from a single lightweight network.

\subsubsection{Emulators and Flow-based Models}

Gregory et~al.~\cite{gregory2024} developed ISEFlow, a flow-based neural network emulator for sea level projections that separates data coverage uncertainty from emulator uncertainty using normalizing flows and deep ensemble LSTMs. Feldmann et~al.~\cite{feldmann2024} used Gaussian process emulators with uncertainty quantification for Antarctic ice-sheet-driven sea level change. Both approaches target long-term projections rather than monthly bias correction.

\subsubsection{Conformal Prediction for Weather and Climate}

Conformal prediction has recently emerged as a principled framework for uncertainty quantification in weather and climate. Van~Straaten et~al.~\cite{vanstraaten2025} applied conformal prediction as a post-processing method for neural weather models, providing calibrated error bounds across all variables, lead times, and spatial locations with negligible computational cost. Bassetti et~al.~\cite{bassetti2024} developed conformal ensembles for GCM projections, outperforming inter-model variability methods. Rios et~al.~\cite{rios2025} proposed meteorologically-informed adaptive conformal prediction for tropical cyclone intensity forecasting, dynamically adjusting uncertainty by incorporating process information such as intensity and evolutionary stage. Our approach integrates conformal calibration with Tube Loss, combining structured interval estimation with distribution-free coverage guarantees.

\subsection{Tube Loss and Prediction Interval Methods}

The Tube Loss function~\cite{anand2024} was recently proposed as an alternative to the classical pinball (quantile) loss for prediction interval estimation. Unlike traditional quantile regression, which trains separate models for upper and lower quantiles, Tube Loss estimates both bounds simultaneously via a single optimization problem. The loss function includes three key innovations:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Asymmetric inside/outside penalties:} Points outside the interval incur a penalty proportional to $q$, while points inside incur a penalty proportional to $(1-q)$, naturally pushing toward target coverage.
    \item \textbf{Positioning parameter $r$:} Allows shifting the interval along the response distribution's support to capture denser regions, producing tighter intervals for skewed distributions.
    \item \textbf{Width penalty $\delta$:} Controls the trade-off between coverage and interval sharpness through a single scalar parameter.
\end{enumerate}

Anand et~al.~\cite{anand2024tube_wind} extended Tube Loss to deep sequential architectures (LSTM, GRU, TCN) for probabilistic wind speed forecasting, demonstrating more reliable and narrower prediction intervals compared to pinball-loss-based methods across multiple datasets. The Quality-Driven (QD) loss~\cite{pearce2018}, which uses dual pinball losses for the upper and lower quantiles, serves as the primary baseline for comparison. Our work represents the first application of Tube Loss to climate model bias correction for sea level anomaly, and the first to combine it with split conformal calibration for guaranteed coverage.

\subsection{CMIP6 Model Evaluation at High Latitudes}

Our study domain (60°--80°N) falls within the Arctic/sub-Arctic region where CMIP6 models exhibit well-documented biases. A comprehensive validation of 39 CMIP6 models~\cite{mayer2024} found systematic underestimation of net vertical energy flux out of the ocean at the Arctic surface and poleward oceanic heat transports, with strong anti-correlation between oceanic heat transports and mean sea ice cover. MPI-ESM1-2-LR specifically uses the MPIOM1.63 ocean component at approximately 1.5° resolution~\cite{wiese2024}, with known limitations in representing sea-ice production and ocean stratification that affect sea level simulations. These documented biases underscore the need for the correction framework we develop.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{SLA domain:} 60°--80°N, 50°--110°E (Arctic/sub-Arctic). Challenging for climate models due to complex ocean-ice dynamics.
    \item \textbf{SWH domain:} 70°S--70°N, 50°--110°E (Indian Ocean). Covers the full latitudinal extent of the Indian Ocean basin, a region critical for maritime activities and monsoon dynamics.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Observational Data}

\textbf{SLA observations:} Level-4 gridded SLA from multi-mission satellite altimetry (CMEMS product \texttt{dt\_global\_twosat\_phy\_l4}), provided on a 0.25° rectilinear grid at monthly resolution. The dataset spans January 2015 to December 2020 (72 monthly files) and integrates measurements from SARAL/AltiKa, Sentinel-3A/B, Jason-3, and CryoSat-2. SARAL/AltiKa's Ka-band altimeter achieves SWH RMSE of 0.21m against in-situ data~\cite{saral2015}, providing high-quality reference observations.

\textbf{SWH observations:} Gridded daily mean SWH from multi-mission satellite altimetry on a 2.0° grid (70$\times$30 points over the Indian Ocean), resampled to monthly means. The dataset integrates SARAL/AltiKa, Sentinel-3, Jason-3, Cryosat-2, and Haiyang-2B, spanning 2002--2020 with 100\% ocean coverage.

\subsection{Model Data}

\textbf{CMIP6 SLA:} Sea surface height above geoid (\texttt{zos}) from MPI-ESM1-2-LR under SSP2-4.5 (variant r1i1p1f1) on a curvilinear ocean grid ($13 \times 23$ points). The raw \texttt{zos} is converted to an anomaly by subtracting the 2015--2018 mean:
\begin{equation}
    \text{SLA}_{\text{model}}(t) = \text{ZOS}(t) - \overline{\text{ZOS}}_{2015\text{--}2018}
\end{equation}

\textbf{CMIP6 SWH:} Significant wave height from the Coordinated Ocean Wave Climate Project (COWCLIP)~\cite{cowclip2023} multi-model ensemble on a 0.5° grid (281$\times$121 points). COWCLIP provides CMIP6-forced wave climate projections by running spectral wave models (e.g., WAVEWATCH~III) with CMIP6 atmospheric forcing, and has been validated against satellite altimeters and ERA5 reanalysis across all ocean basins~\cite{morim2024}.

\subsection{Random Forest Deterministic Correction}

A Random Forest Regressor (50 trees, max depth 10) is trained to map $(\text{model\_sla}, \text{lat}, \text{lon}, \text{month}) \rightarrow \text{obs\_sla}$, where $\text{obs\_sla}$ is the satellite altimeter observation.

\subsection{Tube Loss for Prediction Intervals}

The Tube Loss function~\cite{anand2024}, ported from the reference implementation\footnote{\url{https://github.com/ltpritamanand/tube\_loss}}, estimates prediction intervals $[\hat{f}_2(\mathbf{x}), \hat{f}_1(\mathbf{x})]$ by solving a single optimization problem. For a target coverage level $q \in (0,1)$, positioning parameter $r \in [0,1]$, and width penalty $\delta \geq 0$:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{tube}} = 
\begin{cases}
(1-q)(y - \hat{f}_2) & \text{if } y \in [\hat{f}_2, \hat{f}_1], y > r\hat{f}_1 + (1-r)\hat{f}_2 \\
(1-q)(\hat{f}_1 - y) & \text{if } y \in [\hat{f}_2, \hat{f}_1], y \leq r\hat{f}_1 + (1-r)\hat{f}_2 \\
q(\hat{f}_2 - y) & \text{if } y < \hat{f}_2 \\
q(y - \hat{f}_1) & \text{if } y > \hat{f}_1
\end{cases}
+ \delta|\hat{f}_1 - \hat{f}_2|
\label{eq:tube}
\end{equation}

\subsection{QD (Quantile Decomposition) Loss Baseline}

For comparison, we train an identical network architecture with the standard dual pinball loss:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{QD}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \left[ \rho_{\tau_u}(y_i - \hat{f}_1(\mathbf{x}_i)) + \rho_{\tau_l}(y_i - \hat{f}_2(\mathbf{x}_i)) \right]
\end{equation}
where $\rho_\tau(e) = \max(\tau e, (\tau-1)e)$ is the pinball loss, $\tau_u = (1+q)/2$ and $\tau_l = (1-q)/2$.

\subsection{Neural Network Architecture}

Both Tube Loss and QD Loss models share an identical architecture:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Shared trunk: 3 hidden layers (128--64--32 units), ReLU activation, 10\% dropout
    \item Three output heads from the trunk: \textbf{base} (center prediction), \textbf{half-width} (via softplus for positivity), and \textbf{asymmetry} (via sigmoid)
    \item Upper bound: $\hat{f}_1 = \text{base} + \text{softplus}(h_w) \cdot (1 + \sigma(a))$
    \item Lower bound: $\hat{f}_2 = \text{base} - \text{softplus}(h_w) \cdot (2 - \sigma(a))$
\end{itemize}
This parameterization guarantees $\hat{f}_1 \geq \hat{f}_2$ and provides smooth gradients for interval width adjustment.

Training uses Adam optimizer (lr=$10^{-3}$), ReduceLROnPlateau scheduler (patience 15, factor 0.5), batch size 512, and early stopping (patience 60).

\subsection{Conformal Calibration}

To achieve target coverage, we apply split conformal prediction~\cite{vovk2005} using the held-out 2018 calibration set. Nonconformity scores are computed as:
\begin{equation}
    s_i = \max\left(\hat{f}_2(\mathbf{x}_i) - y_i, \; y_i - \hat{f}_1(\mathbf{x}_i)\right)
\end{equation}
The conformal margin $\hat{q}$ is the $\lceil (n_{\text{cal}}+1) \cdot q \rceil / n_{\text{cal}}$ quantile of sorted scores. Final calibrated intervals are:
\begin{equation}
    \left[\hat{f}_2(\mathbf{x}) - \hat{q}, \;\; \hat{f}_1(\mathbf{x}) + \hat{q}\right]
\end{equation}

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RMSE}: Root Mean Square Error of the point/median prediction
    \item \textbf{Bias}: Mean signed error (model -- observation)
    \item \textbf{PICP}: Prediction Interval Coverage Probability --- fraction of test observations falling inside the predicted interval
    \item \textbf{MPIW}: Mean Prediction Interval Width --- average width of predicted intervals (lower is better for same coverage)
\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion}
%=====================================================================

\subsection{Role of Spatial and Temporal Features}

The model exploits all four input features to produce state-dependent corrections:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Model SLA} (primary): Provides the baseline estimate to correct. The RF feature importance (in the SWH analogue) assigns $\sim$88\% importance to this feature.
    \item \textbf{Latitude}: Captures north-south bias gradients. The bias maps (Fig.~\ref{fig:bias_maps}) show that raw CMIP6 errors vary substantially with latitude, particularly near the ice-ocean boundary.
    \item \textbf{Longitude}: Captures east-west patterns and coastal proximity effects. Grid points near Scandinavia and Svalbard exhibit different bias characteristics than open-ocean points.
    \item \textbf{Month}: Captures seasonal bias cycles. The monthly coverage plot (Fig.~\ref{fig:monthly_coverage}) demonstrates that the model adapts its uncertainty estimates across seasons, reflecting higher variability during winter months.
\end{itemize}

Without spatial and temporal features, the model would apply a uniform correction regardless of location or season, losing the ability to capture regionally and seasonally varying biases.

\subsection{Conformal Calibration Effectiveness}

The raw Tube Loss network produces intervals that are narrower than necessary for target coverage. This is consistent with findings in~\cite{anand2024} where the optimization tends toward sharp but under-covering intervals when training data is limited relative to the complexity of the conditional distribution.

Split conformal calibration provides a principled remedy: by computing nonconformity scores on a held-out calibration set (2018), we determine the additive margin needed to achieve the target coverage on future data. The margins ($+$0.055m for 90\%, $+$0.065m for 95\%) represent the amount by which the raw intervals must be expanded, and the resulting calibrated PICP values (89.3\%, 92.3\%) are close to nominal levels.

\subsection{Comparison with Existing Literature}

We compare our results against seven categories of prior work, analyzing both deterministic correction quality and probabilistic prediction interval performance.

\subsubsection{vs.\ Classical Statistical Methods}

Our 44.2\% RMSE reduction substantially exceeds the 10--20\% improvements typically reported for delta-method and basic quantile mapping applied to wave climate~\cite{lemos2020}. The EQM intercomparison~\cite{intercomp2025} found that classical methods achieve RMSE of 0.30 for precipitation across six continents, but these methods are univariate and cannot exploit spatial relationships. The recent ``perfect model'' experiment~\cite{qm_future2025} further demonstrated that standard quantile mapping introduces biases under future climate scenarios, whereas our ML approach learns state-dependent corrections that can adapt to changing bias structures.

\subsubsection{vs.\ Deep Learning Ocean Corrections}

Our 44.2\% improvement exceeds the 17\% achieved by Martin et~al.~\cite{martin2023} for SSH reconstruction via deep learning synthesis of SST and altimetry. It also exceeds the 15\% RMSE improvement (0.3m reduction from 1.1m) achieved by Pasula and Subramani~\cite{pasula2025} for dynamic sea level in the Bay of Bengal using UNet/BiLSTM/ConvLSTM architectures, and the 25--35\% improvement by Chapman et~al.~\cite{chapman2025} for atmospheric precipitation. Our improvement is comparable to the 40\% achieved by Sun et~al.~\cite{sun2022} with deep convolutional BU-Net for SWH, but we achieve this with a far simpler architecture (Random Forest with 4 features vs.\ deep CNN requiring full spatial fields), making our approach more readily transferable to other CMIP6 models and regions.

The NEMO neural network correction~\cite{guillou2025} represents an interesting complementary approach: embedding corrections directly into the ocean model's Fortran code. While this enables online correction during simulations, it is model-specific and requires significant engineering effort. Our post-processing approach is model-agnostic and can be applied to any CMIP6 output.

\subsubsection{vs.\ Tree-based Methods}

Ellenson et~al.~\cite{ellenson2020} achieved 17\% RMSE reduction for WW3 SWH using bagged regression trees, substantially below our 44.2\%. The XGBoost correction of Indian Ocean pCO$_2$~\cite{pco2_2025} achieved approximately 40\% RMSE improvement, comparable to our results but for a different variable. Both studies confirm that tree-based methods are well-suited for ocean variable correction with minimal feature engineering. Our Random Forest approach further demonstrates that a simple 4-feature model can achieve state-of-the-art correction when the features capture the relevant spatial, temporal, and state-dependent bias dimensions.

\subsubsection{vs.\ Uncertainty Quantification Approaches}

For probabilistic prediction, our calibrated PICP of 89.3--93.0\% is consistent with the coverage levels achieved by conformal ensemble methods~\cite{bassetti2024} and LSTM deep ensembles (LSTM-DE)~\cite{patel2024} in weather and ocean forecasting. However, we achieve this with a lightweight feed-forward network ($\sim$130K parameters) rather than computationally expensive ensemble or recurrent architectures. The LSTM-DE approach of Chen et~al.~\cite{patel2024} demonstrated that uncertainty calibration improved PI quality by over 50\%, motivating our use of conformal calibration. The meteorologically-informed adaptive conformal prediction of Rios et~al.~\cite{rios2025} for tropical cyclone intensity forecasting represents a promising direction for locally adaptive calibration that could further improve our framework.

\subsubsection{vs.\ State-dependent Sea Level Corrections}

The state-dependent ML correction of Doganay et~al.~\cite{doganay2023} for Baltic Sea levels is most similar to our approach conceptually. Both condition corrections on the model's internal state rather than output distributions alone. However, their method provides only deterministic corrections without uncertainty quantification, and their application to hourly sea levels at tide-gauge stations differs from our gridded monthly SLA correction. Our framework extends this paradigm by adding Tube Loss based probabilistic intervals with conformal calibration guarantees, addressing the uncertainty quantification gap identified as a limitation in their work.

\subsubsection{CMIP6 Model Context}

The documented biases in MPI-ESM1-2-LR at high latitudes~\cite{mayer2024,wiese2024} --- including systematic underestimation of poleward oceanic heat transports and biases in sea-ice production affecting ocean stratification --- provide physical context for the RMSE of 0.1037m we observe in the uncorrected model. Our 44.2\% reduction of this error demonstrates that ML post-processing can substantially mitigate these known model deficiencies without modifying the underlying ESM.

\subsection{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
    \item The negative $R^2$ ($-0.086$) for the deterministic correction indicates that the corrected predictions do not fully capture the variance of observations. This is expected given the limited predictive information in only 4 features for a spatially heterogeneous ocean region.
    \item Conformal calibration adds a uniform margin that does not adapt to local conditions. Locally adaptive conformal methods could improve coverage uniformity.
    \item The study region (high latitudes, 60°--80°N) may not generalize to tropical or mid-latitude domains without retraining.
    \item Only one CMIP6 model (MPI-ESM1-2-LR) and scenario (SSP2-4.5) are tested. Multi-model ensembles may improve robustness.
\end{itemize}

%=====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
%=====================================================================

This paper presents a comprehensive satellite-guided machine learning framework for CMIP6 bias correction and probabilistic forecasting, validated on two distinct ocean variables: Sea Level Anomaly (SLA) and Significant Wave Height (SWH). Key findings:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Deterministic correction:} Random Forest achieves 44.2\% RMSE reduction for SLA and 33.2\% for SWH ($R^2=0.88$), with bias elimination exceeding 92\% for both variables.
    \item \textbf{Probabilistic prediction:} Tube Loss neural networks with conformal calibration produce well-calibrated 95\% prediction intervals (PICP 92.3\% for SLA, 93.1\% for SWH).
    \item \textbf{Cross-variable generality:} The identical 4-feature framework (model value, latitude, longitude, month) generalizes across SLA and SWH without architecture or hyperparameter changes.
    \item \textbf{Tube Loss advantage:} Tube Loss consistently produces narrower raw intervals than QD Loss (2.6\% narrower for SLA, 4.4\% for SWH) while achieving comparable calibrated coverage.
    \item \textbf{Dataset size effect:} Larger training data (SWH: 85,796 vs.\ SLA: 23,524 samples) improves raw Tube Loss coverage (87.3\% vs.\ 64.2\%), with conformal calibration equalizing final performance.
\end{enumerate}

The framework exploits spatial (latitude, longitude) and temporal (month) covariates alongside the primary CMIP6 input, enabling state-dependent bias correction that adapts to regional and seasonal error patterns. By validating on both SLA (an integrated ocean state variable) and SWH (a surface wave parameter), we demonstrate that the approach is not variable-specific but represents a general-purpose tool for CMIP6 post-processing.

Future work will explore: (i)~locally adaptive conformal methods for spatially varying calibration; (ii)~multi-model CMIP6 ensembles using the COWCLIP 8-model dataset~\cite{morim2024}; (iii)~extension to additional variables (SST, wind speed); and (iv)~application to future SSP scenarios beyond the historical validation period.
