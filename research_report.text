Data and Study Region

We analyze bias correction of CMIP6 significant wave height (SWH) projections over the Indian Ocean (50–110°E, 70°S–70°N) on a common 2° grid (monthly means, 2015–2020) using multi-mission satellite altimetry as “observations”. The Indian Ocean has a highly non-stationary wave climate: the Northern Indian Ocean experiences reversing monsoonal winds (SW monsoon in boreal summer, NE monsoon in winter) that drive large seasonal SWH changes, while the Southern Indian Ocean is dominated by high-energy swell from mid-latitudes. For example, summer monsoon waves in the Arabian Sea and Bay of Bengal reach several meters, whereas the Southern Ocean produces persistent swells. Multi-mission altimeters (e.g. ESA/CCI, NOAA) provide gridded SWH products at 2° resolution; for instance, ERA5 wave heights agree with calibrated altimetry with correlation ~0.97 (RMSE ~0.2–0.4 m), so altimetry is taken as the reference “truth” for bias correction.

Traditional Statistical Bias Correction (Quantile Mapping)

Quantile mapping (QM) is the standard univariate bias-correction method. It works by constructing a transfer function that remaps the cumulative distribution of model SWH to that of the observations. In practice, one fits either an empirical or parametric CDF to the historical model output and adjusts future model values to match the observed quantiles. QM has the advantage of forcing the corrected data to exactly match the observed climatology (means, variances, and quantiles) over the calibration period. It is straightforward to implement and ensures that the long-term mean and distribution of SWH agree with the reference dataset.

Limitations of QM: Because QM is typically applied independently at each grid point (and usually to one variable at a time), it has several drawbacks. It does not account for spatial or inter-variable relationships. In fact, studies warn that univariate bias correction can break physical consistency and inter-variable correlations. For example, applying separate QM to wind and waves might violate energy balance or spatial coherence. Quantile methods also assume a stationary bias: the transfer function derived from 2015–2020 climatology is applied unchanged to future periods. This can distort trends or seasonal cycles if the underlying model-observation relationship changes. Importantly, QM often struggles with extreme tails. It relies on adequate sampling of rare events, and even then the tails of model and observed distributions may differ in shape. As a result, quantile mapping may under- or over-correct extremes. Some studies note that QM yields good mean-statistics (e.g. reduced RMSE) but can fail to capture the true frequency of very high waves. In summary:

Pros: Guarantees matched climatological mean and CDF (including extremes if data-rich), simple to apply .

Cons: Univariate (breaks variable covariances), assumes stationary bias (may distort trends), and can misrepresent rare extreme quantiles.

Machine Learning Methods

We compare two data-driven bias-correction approaches: (1) Random Forest (RF) – a classical ensemble-tree model, and (2) Deep Learning (CNNs/U-Net) – convolutional neural networks operating on gridded fields.

Random Forest Bias Correction

A Random Forest regressor learns a mapping from input features to the bias (model minus observed SWH). In practice, one can train RF on tabular data where each training sample corresponds to a grid cell (and time) with features such as the raw CMIP6 SWH and possibly ancillary variables (latitude, season, model winds, etc.) and the target residual. After training, RF predicts and subtracts the bias from new model output.

Computational efficiency: RF is very lightweight. It can be trained on a standard CPU with modest memory because it only processes 1D feature vectors, not large images. Training time scales roughly linearly with the number of grid points and trees. For example, in climate bias-correction tests, RF models have been trained in minutes to hours on typical PCs. No GPU is required.

Performance: RF captures complex nonlinear relationships and interactions in the data without requiring specification of a parametric form. In climate tasks RF often outperforms simpler regressions or even neural nets. For instance, Holthuijsen et al. (2023) found that a Random Forest correction for temperature errors reduced RMSE from 2.90°C to 0.44°C, far outperforming linear regression or neural network models. This suggests RF can substantially improve bias correction accuracy when enough training data is available.

Handling spatial/temporal variability: By default, RF treats each sample independently and does not “see” the spatial grid. Spatial coherence can only be captured if features like latitude/longitude or spatial indices are explicitly included. Temporal non-stationarity (e.g. seasonality) must similarly be encoded via input features (month index, seasonal flags). RF does not inherently model time series. Its simplicity means it may not fully exploit spatiotemporal patterns unless carefully designed.

Overfitting and robustness: RF is ensemble-based and tends to be robust to overfitting, but deep trees can still overfit noise if not tuned. It handles mixed data types well. However, RF provides little insight into physical consistency: it makes pointwise corrections without enforcing conservation laws. If model biases change (concept drift), the forest may need retraining on new data.

Summary (RF): Random Forests offer a fast, easy-to-train bias corrector that often yields better accuracy than simple methods. They are ideal when computational resources are limited. The downside is that RF acts like a “black-box” interpolator on independent samples: it may ignore spatial patterns or physics, and it assumes the training bias relationships remain valid in future periods.

Deep Learning (CNN/U-Net)

Convolutional neural networks treat the bias correction as a spatiotemporal mapping of whole fields. For example, one might feed an entire 2°×2° gridded map of CMIP6 SWH (or the 3D stack of SWH and ancillary fields) into a CNN/U-Net that outputs a correction field of the same dimensions. A U-Net architecture (an encoder-decoder CNN with skip connections) is well-suited to capture multi-scale spatial features while preserving fine details.

Spatial structure: CNNs excel at exploiting spatial coherence. Convolutional filters can learn regional patterns (e.g. the effect of monsoon winds in the Arabian Sea) and ensure nearby grid points have smoothly varying corrections. U-Nets can incorporate context at multiple scales (local vs basin-wide). This is a key advantage over RF: the model “sees” the full 2D field and can learn that certain biases are geographically connected.

Incorporating physics/ancillary data: CNNs can easily take multiple input channels (e.g. SWH plus wind fields or seasonal variables). This allows learning bias as a function of both wave state and wind forcing. For instance, Zhang et al. (2024) built a spatiotemporal deep-learning model for SWH that included both wave and wind fields; this co-driven model outperformed a wave-only version, highlighting the benefit of multivariate input.

Capturing extremes: Deep nets can focus on extreme events by using specialized loss functions. In Zhang et al. (2024), a novel “pixel-switch” loss dynamically emphasized grid points with large SWH bias, which led to significant error reductions under both normal and extreme conditions. In general, ML models can be trained to pay extra attention to tail events if the loss is designed accordingly.

Computational cost: The flip side is resource intensity. Training a CNN or U-Net on global 2° data for 6 years of monthly SWH would require processing 60×90=5,400 grid points over ~72 months, potentially as 5,400×72 training samples (plus augmentations). A modest U-Net with millions of parameters would likely need hundreds of training epochs for convergence. This requires high-end GPUs and can take many hours or days. Inference (applying the trained network) is fast on GPU but still heavier than an RF pass. Deep models also need careful tuning (architecture, learning rates, regularization) to avoid overfitting. Large model capacity means one risks fitting noise if the training set is not sufficiently large or diverse.

Generalization: CNNs tend to generalize better spatially (due to weight sharing) but can still suffer if the data distribution shifts. If CMIP6 biases under a future scenario have patterns not seen in 2015–2020, the network may produce unreliable corrections. Deep nets have many degrees of freedom, so they may memorize spurious relationships. Nonetheless, with enough data and augmentation (e.g. training separate season-specific models), CNNs can in principle learn the non-stationary patterns of monsoon vs non-monsoon seasons.

Summary (CNN/U-Net): Deep convolutional bias-correction models can capture complex spatiotemporal patterns and potentially handle extreme values more flexibly (with tailored loss functions). They are naturally multivariate and can enforce local smoothness. However, their computational overhead is very high, and they require large training datasets and expertise to train. Without physics constraints, they may still produce unphysical artifacts unless guided.

Computational Efficiency Trade-offs

A primary design goal is efficiency. Random Forests are orders of magnitude cheaper to train and run than CNNs. An RF trained on tabular input (e.g. one feature per grid point) can be fit in seconds–minutes on a laptop, and makes predictions in milliseconds per sample. No GPUs are needed. In contrast, CNN/U-Net models ingest entire 2D fields (and possibly sequences in time), so even one forward/backward pass touches millions of weights. For example, a single epoch of training on a U-Net with 10<sup>6</sup> parameters over ~5,400 pixels ×72 months would require on the order of 10<sup>9</sup> multiply-adds; hundreds of epochs multiply that. In practice this means many GPU-hours. Deep models also demand more memory (to hold intermediate feature maps) and suffer longer tuning cycles.

By comparison, RF complexity scales with (number of trees)×(log of training samples), which is trivial here. Studies consistently find tree ensembles to train much faster than deep nets for similar tasks. For instance, in the temperature bias-correction example, the RF model was developed and evaluated with a few lines of code, whereas a CNN would require substantial engineering effort and runtime. Thus, if computational resources are constrained, RF is strongly favored. However, one should balance this against potential accuracy gains: if a CNN produces markedly better bias correction (especially for extremes), the heavy cost might be justified for a final climate-change projection task.

Physical Consistency and Generalization

A critical issue is whether the correction preserves physical realism. Statistical BC methods lack physical constraints by design. They adjust SWH to match observations without regard to underlying wave dynamics. For example, a QM or pure ML model might alter the geographic pattern of waves in ways that violate known relationships (e.g. energy cannot flow uphill in frequency space). Sampson et al. (2017) highlight that common BC methods “lack a sound physical basis” and can introduce unphysical inconsistencies between variables. In waves, this could mean a corrected SWH field that no longer corresponds to any plausible wind forcing.

Random Forests can include physics-related input features (wind speed, fetch, seasonal cycle), which indirectly infuses some physical context. But RF does not enforce conservation laws or geophysical constraints. A large RF tree might predict SWH corrections that reduce bias, but there is no guarantee the adjusted SWH is consistent with other climate variables or processes. Similarly, CNNs/U-Nets can learn to exploit spatial relationships, but without explicit guidance they may generate physically inconsistent outputs (e.g. sharp gradients or improbable wave trains).

This motivates Physics-Informed ML (PIML) or hybrid approaches. In a PIML framework, known physics are built into the loss function or architecture. For example, Adigun et al. (2026) developed a “physics-constrained” deep learning model for CMIP6 solar radiation: they embedded radiative transfer equations into the network, which preserved energy balance and improved correction accuracy dramatically. They report reducing MAE by ~70–85% while raising correlations to >0.97. Such approaches show that enforcing physics can yield more reliable corrections. In our wave context, analogous constraints might include the dispersion relation (linking frequency and wavenumber), spectral shape assumptions, or co-variability with winds. Hybrid techniques could also combine statistical and ML steps: e.g. first apply QM to the bulk SWH distribution, then train an RF to correct the residuals (as in Eekhout et al. 2024 for precipitation). Indeed, coupling QM with RF has been shown to out-perform either alone.

Generalizability: Both RF and CNN assume that future bias patterns lie within the training data distribution. Yet climate change or decadal shifts can alter monsoon strength and circulation patterns in 2050–2100. Traditional QM implicitly assumes stationarity of the bias, which is often violated. ML methods likewise can suffer “concept drift” if the underlying distribution changes: a neural net trained on 2015–2020 patterns may become outdated as the climate evolves. Careful design (e.g. training on multiple eras, using climate predictors, or periodically retraining) is needed to maintain performance under non-stationarity.

Extremes and Tail Behavior

Coastal risk analysis demands accurate representation of high-wave events. Quantile mapping guarantees that model-simulated extremes match the observed frequency during the training period (since it remaps high quantiles by definition). However, QM can underperform when the model’s extreme value distribution differs fundamentally from observations. If, for instance, the model never produces a true 99.9th percentile wave seen in observations, QM has little information to correctly project such extremes. Moreover, QM does not add new information beyond the calibration data, so it cannot anticipate unprecedented extremes under future climate change.

Machine learning methods offer alternative ways to handle tails. A CNN or RF can be trained with a loss function that emphasizes large errors (e.g. quantile loss, Huber loss, or custom “pixel-switch” loss) to preferentially fit the tail. The deep-SWH study by Zhang et al. used such a loss to reduce large SWH biases, reporting significant skill improvements for extremes as well as normal conditions. In practice, one might explicitly augment the training set with extreme-event cases (e.g. waveform composites of El Niño winters) to teach the ML model these regimes.

However, ML does not automatically guarantee better extremes. If the training period (2015–2020) contains relatively few truly extreme months for certain locations, a CNN might effectively smooth the output and under-predict rare peaks. It is therefore crucial to evaluate the upper-tail performance (e.g. via Q–Q plots). Empirically, studies of related variables (e.g. precipitation) have found that QM excels at aligning tails when adequate data exist, whereas ML can either do better or worse depending on how extremes are weighted. A potential hybrid strategy is to apply QM to correct the distribution tails and use ML to correct the mean and spatial structure.

Upper-tail trade-off: In summary, QM enforces correct tail probabilities in a statistical sense but can fail if data are sparse. ML methods can learn to represent extremes from complex inputs, but risk under-fitting rare events. The choice of technique may ultimately depend on which replicates the observed Q–Q relation better. Our Q–Q plot analysis should check whether RF or CNN biased corrections preserve the linearity at the highest quantiles (as quantile mapping does by construction) or if they introduce divergence.

Hybrid and Physics-Informed Approaches

Given the strengths and weaknesses above, hybrid methods are promising. For example, combining QM with ML has shown benefits: Eekhout et al. (2024) applied quantile mapping followed by Random Forest to CMIP6 precipitation and found large error reductions beyond QM alone. One could analogously apply QM to align the bulk SWH distribution and then train an RF or CNN on the residuals to capture remaining spatial biases. This leverages QM’s guarantee on distribution shape with ML’s flexibility on structure.

Separately, physics-informed ML (PIML) directly integrates physical laws. While this is cutting-edge for climate bias correction, initial successes (like the solar radiation example) indicate large potential. For wave SWH, a PIML model might enforce relationships such as consistency between wave energy and wind work, or ensure that conservation of mass/energy holds across the field. Embedding oceanographic understanding (e.g. fetch-limited growth functions) could constrain ML predictions and improve extrapolation to unseen conditions. Although implementing PIML is complex, it is an active research frontier that could yield bias corrections that are both accurate and physically credible.

Conclusions

In bias-correcting CMIP6 wave heights, Random Forest and Deep Learning represent two ends of a spectrum. RF is a lightweight, interpretable method: it can be trained on CPUs, quickly applied to new data, and often outperforms simple alternatives. It works well for correcting mean climate errors and requires minimal computational overhead. However, it may not fully capture spatial context or obey physical constraints. CNN/U-Net architectures, by contrast, can capture complex spatiotemporal non-stationarity (e.g. monsoon vs swell regions) and be tuned to focus on extreme tails. They offer higher representational power but at a steep computational cost: GPU training on large gridded datasets and careful regularization are needed. Traditional Quantile Mapping remains a strong baseline for univariate correction of distributions, guaranteeing statistical consistency of means and variances, but it can break physical consistency and may misrepresent tails when data are limited.

Trade-offs: If efficiency and simplicity are paramount (e.g. operational use on limited hardware), RF is appealing. If maximal accuracy (especially for extremes) is required and resources permit, deep CNNs or hybrid PIML models may be justified. In either case, hybrid strategies that marry statistics and ML (e.g. QM+RF) or embed physics (PIML) seem most promising for reliable long-term projections. Finally, continual evaluation on independence data (e.g. post-2020 hindcasts) and thorough Q–Q diagnostics are essential to ensure that any bias-correction method generalizes well to future climates and extreme-event risk assessment.

Machine-Learning vs. Traditional Bias Correction for Wave Climate

Traditional quantile-mapping (QM) methods remain the staple for climate bias adjustment. QM corrects the full distribution of model output to match observed distributions, which is particularly useful for extreme (upper-tail) events. In the context of significant wave height (SWH), Lemos et al. (2019) emphasize that extreme waves – though rare – have disproportionate coastal impacts and thus require a quantile‐based correction. By construction, QM can adjust both mean bias and skewed tails, yielding reliable climatologies of SWH. Indeed, recent reviews note that quantile-mapping is the only method consistently “sufficiently reliable” for large-scale wind-wave resource assessments.

However, QM has well-known limitations. It operates independently at each grid point, ignoring any spatial or inter-variable relationships. As Li et al. (2025) point out, QM “fails to preserve spatial coherence by neglecting inter-grid relationships”, so corrected fields can exhibit unrealistic spatial patterns. QM also inherently assumes that model biases are stationary over time: future climate corrections are applied under the strong hypothesis that present-day biases persist unchanged. In practice this can distort climate signals (by “shifting” trends) and break the physical consistency among variables. In summary, QM is computationally trivial and effective at distributional matching (especially extremes) but neglects spatial context and assumes fixed biases.

Random Forest Bias Correction

Random forests (RF) represent a lightweight machine-learning alternative. RFs are tree-based regression ensembles that can fit complex nonlinear biases at each grid cell or across locations. In practice, RF models for SWH bias might use predictors such as the raw CMIP6 SWH, month/season indicators, latitude/longitude, and large-scale indices (e.g. ENSO/IOD) to predict the bias relative to altimetry. RF is attractive because it is highly efficient: training on tabular data can be done on a standard CPU without GPUs, and inference is very fast. (One presentation even notes that “RF has been found to work well for regression purposes” in climate bias tasks.) In terms of trend and climatology, an RF can learn mean biases and seasonal variations if supplied with the right features (e.g. month, local static features, or climate indices). Because RF does not assume any particular functional form, it can often achieve lower mean bias than simple linear corrections or univariate QM, as long as the training data (2015–2020 in this case) are representative of the biases.

Advantages of RF:

Computational efficiency: RFs use relatively few parameters (trees and splits) and do not need intensive GPU training. This “lightweight” approach makes RF feasible on modest hardware.

Nonlinearity & flexibility: RF can capture complex bias relationships (e.g. between model winds and wave height bias) without strong assumptions.

Mixed features: One can feed RF both model output and ancillary features (e.g. month, large-scale climate modes, even neighboring grid values) to capture some spatiotemporal context.

Limitations of RF:

Poor spatial coherence: Unlike CNNs, RF has no built-in notion of locality or spatial smoothness. If each grid cell is corrected independently, the adjusted SWH field can become noisy or inconsistent. RF can partially overcome this by including neighboring values as features, but this quickly increases dimensionality.

Physical consistency: RF corrects SWH in isolation, with no guarantee that wind-wave physics or inter-variable balances are preserved. For example, wave heights could be corrected independently of corresponding wind speeds or wave directions, potentially producing unrealistic states.

Generalizability: With only ~72 months of training data, RF risks overfitting to the calibration period. The model will assume that biases seen in 2015–2020 persist into the future climate. If climate change alters storm patterns or monsoon strength, the RF correction (like any purely statistical method) may not adapt.

Extremes: RF tends to regress toward the mean for rare events. If the training data has few extreme waves, the RF may systematically under-correct or underpredict the upper tail. Hybrid strategies can help (see below), but a vanilla RF often fails to fully match extreme quantiles.

In practice, combining RF with distributional methods can mitigate some issues. For example, Raeesi et al. (2024) find for precipitation that pure QM left large errors (RMSE ~431 mm/yr), while a hybrid QM+RF roughly halved that error (~198 mm/yr). By analogy, a QM+RF scheme for SWH could use QM to align the bulk and tails of the distribution, then use RF to correct any residual spatial or seasonal biases.

Deep Learning (CNN/U-Net) Bias Correction

Deep convolutional networks (CNNs) and U-Net architectures bring full spatiotemporal modeling to bias correction. A CNN can ingest the gridded CMIP6 SWH fields (potentially alongside other fields, over multiple time steps) and learn spatial patterns of error. By exploiting local convolutional filters, CNNs can automatically learn to adjust features like monsoon swells in the Arabian Sea or wave bands in the Southern Ocean. U-Nets, with their encoder-decoder and skip connections, are especially powerful at preserving large-scale structure while refining local detail. Such architectures have shown remarkable performance: for example, Li et al. (2025) use a generative U-Net model to downscale and bias-correct climate fields simultaneously, achieving over 70% reduction in climatological bias for multiple variables while “significantly enhancing the representation of extremes”. Similarly, the UFNet model (a U-Net combined with a dense network for statistics) was explicitly designed to match higher-order moments, and it “significantly improves the underestimation of extreme precipitation values” relative to simpler correction schemes. These successes suggest that with enough data and design effort, CNN-based methods can capture both mean shifts and tail behavior better than simpler approaches.

Advantages of CNNs/U-Nets:

Spatial coherence: Convolutions ensure that nearby grid points are corrected in a physically smooth way. CNNs inherently learn spatial patterns (e.g. contiguous storm systems), preserving realistic spatial structure.

Joint multivariate correction: Deep networks can accept multiple input channels (e.g. SWH, wind speed, wave period) and learn corrections that respect inter-variable relationships. This can improve physical consistency if trained carefully.

Flexibility to capture complexity: Complex architectures (including ConvLSTM or 3D/4D convolutions) can model evolving patterns and seasonality jointly, potentially capturing non-stationarity better than univariate methods.

Extreme-tail matching: Advanced loss functions (e.g. Q-Q loss) or hybrid networks (like UFNet) can explicitly focus on extreme values, leading to better tail calibration.

Limitations of CNNs:

Computational cost: CNNs and U-Nets have millions of parameters and require GPUs or large-memory machines for training. Training on global (or even basin-wide) data over many time steps is computationally intensive. For example, Li et al. (2025) train a complex generative model for global fields, which is far heavier than a tabular RF on monthly means. In an efficiency-focused context, this overhead is a major drawback.

Data hunger and overfitting: Our dataset is only six years of monthly data. CNNs typically demand much larger datasets to generalize. With limited samples, there is risk of overfitting to the training period. This makes deployment tricky for future climates unless augmented data or transfer learning is used.

Interpretability and uncertainty: Deep nets are “black boxes” and it is hard to quantify uncertainty or ensure they won’t fail in untested regimes. They may fit the training distribution well but produce spurious corrections under new conditions.

Physical consistency: Although CNNs can jointly correct multiple fields, nothing in a vanilla CNN forces adherence to physical laws (e.g. wave energy conservation). Without additional constraints, a deep model could produce biases that conflict with physics.

Hybrid and Physics-Informed Methods

To balance these trade-offs, hybrid approaches are gaining interest. Two promising directions are (a) statistical-ML hybrids and (b) physics-informed ML (PIML).

Statistical-ML hybrids: These methods combine the strengths of QM and ML. For instance, one may apply quantile mapping to match overall distributions, then use RF or a shallow neural net to correct any remaining systematic errors. The Raeesi et al. (2024) study exemplifies this: after QM alone left large precipitation biases, adding an RF stage cut RMSE in half. In SWH, a similar two-step scheme could ensure extremes are aligned (QM) while still tuning mean bias and seasonal patterns (RF). Such hybrids require more effort but can yield superior robustness and smaller residual errors than either method alone.

Physics-informed ML: A more recent trend is to embed known physics into the learning model. For example, Adigun et al. (2026) incorporate radiative-transfer physics into a deep network correcting CMIP6 solar radiation, thereby “maintaining physical consistency between atmospheric variables” and dramatically cutting biases (by ~70–85%). In the wave context, one could enforce, say, conservation of wave action or realistic wave-generation relationships. Other deep-learning bias-correction models are exploring generative adversarial networks or adding loss terms that penalize violations of known constraints. Such PIML approaches promise the best of both worlds: the accuracy of deep networks with built-in constraints that keep outputs physically plausible. The Li et al. (2025) multivariate downscaling model is one example: it simultaneously corrects multiple fields (e.g. sea-surface height, SST, wind) and by doing so improves large-scale patterns like the Indian Ocean Dipole and ENSO teleconnections.

Spatio-Temporal Non-Stationarity and Extremes

The Indian Ocean region has pronounced seasonal and non-stationary patterns (monsoons, tropical cyclone cycles, Southern Ocean swells). Any bias-correction model must cope with these. Temporal non-stationarity (e.g. changing monsoon strength) is a challenge: QM typically calibrates each month or season separately, but still assumes that future interannual variability behaves like the past. ML models could in principle adapt to changing conditions if they include predictors like time or climate indices; for example, studies show that including an ENSO index in bias-correction models improves extreme rainfall errors by ~30–40%. A similar strategy could help capture monsoon-related variability in SWH.

Spatial non-stationarity (different physics in the Arabian Sea vs. Bay of Bengal vs. Southern Ocean) suggests that a global model might struggle to fit all regimes. RF could train separate models per region (or include region indicators), but CNNs can learn spatially varying filters. However, limited data means there is a risk of spurious spatial variability: e.g. a CNN might interpret a typhoon signal erroneously if none occurred in training. Careful cross-validation and incorporation of known cycles (e.g. IOD/ENSO) are needed.

Finally, extreme events deserve special focus. Both QM and advanced ML aim to correct tail behavior, but do so differently. QM directly maps model and observed quantiles, explicitly adjusting the upper tail. In contrast, standard RF or CNN training (optimizing mean-square error) often under-predicts extremes. Specialized losses or architectures can mitigate this. For example, the UFNet approach jointly trains on mean structure (U-Net) and distribution shape (MLP) to capture rare events. In bias-correction Q–Q plots, a purely data-driven model might linearly underperform at high quantiles, whereas QM and PIML methods can better align the 99th-percentile values. In summary, capturing extremes typically requires either distributional methods (like QM) or specially designed ML losses/models; vanilla RF/CNN tends to leave the tail undershot.

Trade-offs and Recommendations

In summary, random forest offers a low-cost, flexible baseline: it can be trained quickly and will reliably correct mean biases if the training data are representative. However, it sacrifices spatial coherence and tail fidelity. Deep CNNs/U-Nets can produce much more skillful corrections by learning physical patterns (monsoon swells, inter-basin teleconnections) and have demonstrated large bias reductions and better extremes. The flip side is heavy computational cost (GPU training on 4-D fields) and the need for substantial data and tuning. Traditional QM remains robust for distribution correction and especially for extremes, but it ignores spatial structure and assumes fixed biases.

A pragmatic strategy might be to use RF (or gradient boosting) as a fast first step for mean climatology, combined with QM for tails – essentially a hybrid quantile-ML method. For higher fidelity in spatial structure and extremes, one could then explore a CNN or U-Net on a smaller domain or with transfer learning, possibly guided by physics (e.g. enforcing energy constraints). Incorporating physics (PIML) or multi-variate corrections tends to improve consistency: e.g. Lu et al.’s generative model not only slashes bias but also recovers realistic ENSO/IOD patterns. Ultimately, the choice depends on resources and priorities: if computational efficiency and ease of training are paramount, RF-based approaches will win, while applications demanding the most accurate extremes and spatial detail may justify a deep-learning solution (potentially with added physics constraints).
